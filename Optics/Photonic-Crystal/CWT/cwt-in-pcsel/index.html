3D-CWT: the repository

3D-CWT is our open-source Python implementation of three-dimensional coupled-wave theory for square-lattice photonic-crystal surface-emitting lasers (PCSELs). Starting from a layer stack and a photonic-crystal unit cell, the passive optical solver calculates the vertical guided mode, the four-wave coupling matrix, and the modes of a finite cavity using NumPy and SciPy.

· Release notes

The calculation follows this sequence:

Layer stack and hole geometry → averaged vertical waveguide → Fourier coefficients and outgoing Green functions → four-wave coupling matrix → finite-cavity eigenmodes.

This post explains that sequence, beginning with 2D CWT and then introducing the vertical structure and surface radiation in 3D CWT. The theory and numerical results presented here use the 1.3-series passive optical solver, which covers lossless-material, scalar TE calculations on a square lattice. The badge above tracks the latest stable release of the repository; it does not change the version used for the results below. Carrier transport and above-threshold laser dynamics, including active-cavity extensions in later releases, are outside the scope of this article.

The earlier version of this post is retained as a historical note. The equations below use the conventions of the 1.3-series optical implementation.

Introduction to coupled-wave theory

A PCSEL combines in-plane distributed feedback with out-of-plane diffraction. Near the second-order Bragg condition of a square lattice, four counterpropagating guided waves dominate the resonant field. Their interference determines the band-edge modes, while scattering into the normal direction provides surface emission.

Resolving every unit cell of a large-area cavity with FDTD or FEM can be expensive. CWT separates the fast lattice-scale oscillation from the slowly varying cavity envelope. The unit-cell and vertical calculations determine a small coupling matrix; the finite-device calculation then solves for four envelopes across the aperture.

Sakai, Miyai, and Noda developed a square-lattice TE coupled-wave model in 2006.[1] Liang and co-workers subsequently incorporated the vertical waveguide and radiation into a 3D formulation.[2] Liang’s doctoral thesis gives the detailed derivation and finite-cavity examples used here.[3] Later work by Inoue and co-workers further organized Hermitian and non-Hermitian couplings for large-area PCSEL design.[4]

Coupled-wave theory

2D CWT

The in-plane wave equation

First consider a dielectric structure invariant along zzz. For TE polarization, the electric field lies in the xxx–yyy plane and the magnetic field has a scalar component HzH_zHz​. In a nonmagnetic medium,

∂∂x(1ϵ∂Hz∂x)+∂∂y(1ϵ∂Hz∂y)+k02Hz=0,k0=ωc.\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\partial H_z}{\partial x}\right) +\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\partial H_z}{\partial y}\right) +k_0^2H_z=0,\qquad k_0=\frac{\omega}{c}.∂x∂​(ϵ1​∂x∂Hz​​)+∂y∂​(ϵ1​∂y∂Hz​​)+k02​Hz​=0,k0​=cω​.

For the periodic problem at Γ\GammaΓ, expand

Hz=∑phpe−iGp⋅ρ,1ϵ=∑pηpe−iGp⋅ρ.H_z=\sum_{\mathbf p}h_{\mathbf p} \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf G_{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}, \qquad \frac{1}{\epsilon}=\sum_{\mathbf p}\eta_{\mathbf p} \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf G_{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}.Hz​=p∑​hp​e−iGp​⋅ρ,ϵ1​=p∑​ηp​e−iGp​⋅ρ.

Substitution gives the Fourier-space equations

∑q(Gp⋅Gq)ηp−qhq=k02hp.\sum_{\mathbf q} (\mathbf G_{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf G_{\mathbf q}) \eta_{\mathbf p-\mathbf q}h_{\mathbf q} =k_0^2h_{\mathbf p}.q∑​(Gp​⋅Gq​)ηp−q​hq​=k02​hp​.

The inverse-permittivity coefficients ηp\eta_{\mathbf p}ηp​ belong to this magnetic-field formulation. They should not be interchanged with the permittivity coefficients ξp\xi_{\mathbf p}ξp​ used in the electric-field formulation below.

From many harmonics to four waves

Near Bragg resonance, retain the four basic harmonics explicitly:

Hz≃h+xe−iβ0x+h−xe+iβ0x+h+ye−iβ0y+h−ye+iβ0y+auxiliary harmonics.\begin{aligned} H_z\simeq{}&h_{+x}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\beta_0x} +h_{-x}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\beta_0x}\\ &+h_{+y}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\beta_0y} +h_{-y}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\beta_0y} +\text{auxiliary harmonics}. \end{aligned}Hz​≃​h+x​e−iβ0​x+h−x​e+iβ0​x+h+y​e−iβ0​y+h−y​e+iβ0​y+auxiliary harmonics.​

The auxiliary harmonics mediate additional scattering paths. In the early 2D model, the four diagonal orders (±1,±1)(\pm1,\pm1)(±1,±1) provide important indirect coupling between the xxx- and yyy-propagating basic waves. More generally, partition the Fourier system into basic and auxiliary amplitudes:

(MBBMBHMHBMHH)(hBhH)=0.\begin{pmatrix} M_{BB}&M_{BH}\\ M_{HB}&M_{HH} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}h_B\\h_H\end{pmatrix}=0.(MBB​MHB​​MBH​MHH​​)(hB​hH​​)=0.

Eliminating hHh_HhH​ gives

(MBB−MBHMHH−1MHB)hB=0.\left(M_{BB}-M_{BH}M_{HH}^{-1}M_{HB}\right)h_B=0.(MBB​−MBH​MHH−1​MHB​)hB​=0.

This is the central coupled-wave reduction: direct scattering and paths through eliminated harmonics both contribute to an effective four-wave problem. A slowly varying envelope approximation then retains first derivatives of the basic amplitudes and neglects their second derivatives.

The 2D model explains in-plane feedback and band splitting. Its zzz-invariant geometry has no upper or lower radiation boundary. Surface emission must be supplied by an additional model; a quantitative treatment of the layer stack requires the 3D extension.

3D CWT

1. Solve the averaged vertical waveguide

In a multilayer PCSEL, the four basic waves share a reference guided profile Θ0(z)\Theta_0(z)Θ0​(z). It satisfies

d2Θ0dz2+[k02ϵˉ(z)−β2]Θ0=0,∫−∞+∞∣Θ0(z)∣2 dz=1.\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\Theta_0}{\mathrm{d}z^2} +\left[k_0^2\bar\epsilon(z)-\beta^2\right]\Theta_0=0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\Theta_0(z)|^2\,\mathrm{d}z=1.dz2d2Θ0​​+[k02​ϵˉ(z)−β2]Θ0​=0,∫−∞+∞​∣Θ0​(z)∣2dz=1.

The normalization includes the evanescent tails in both claddings. For a specified period, our examples determine the reference frequency by solving

βfundamental(k0)=β0=2πa.\beta_{\mathrm{fundamental}}(k_0)=\beta_0=\frac{2\pi}{a}.βfundamental​(k0​)=β0​=a2π​.

Define the electric-field envelope vector in the fixed order

V=(Rx,Sx,Ry,Sy)T.\mathbf V=(R_x,S_x,R_y,S_y)^{\mathsf T}.V=(Rx​,Sx​,Ry​,Sy​)T.

Its basic-wave contribution is

Ey(B)=Θ0(z)[Rx(x,y)e−iβ0x+Sx(x,y)e+iβ0x],Ex(B)=Θ0(z)[Ry(x,y)e−iβ0y+Sy(x,y)e+iβ0y].\begin{aligned} E_y^{(B)}&=\Theta_0(z) \left[R_x(x,y)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\beta_0x} +S_x(x,y)\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\beta_0x}\right],\\ E_x^{(B)}&=\Theta_0(z) \left[R_y(x,y)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\beta_0y} +S_y(x,y)\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\beta_0y}\right]. \end{aligned}Ey(B)​Ex(B)​​=Θ0​(z)[Rx​(x,y)e−iβ0​x+Sx​(x,y)e+iβ0​x],=Θ0​(z)[Ry​(x,y)e−iβ0​y+Sy​(x,y)e+iβ0​y].​

2. Calculate radiation and auxiliary fields

The radiation and auxiliary harmonics generally have vertical profiles different from Θ0\Theta_0Θ0​. For each in-plane order, introduce an outgoing scalar TE Green function:

[d2dz2+k02ϵˉ(z)−∣Gm,n∣2]Gm,n(z,z′)=−δ(z−z′).\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}z^2} +k_0^2\bar\epsilon(z)-|\mathbf G_{m,n}|^2\right] G_{m,n}(z,z')=-\delta(z-z').[dz2d2​+k02​ϵˉ(z)−∣Gm,n​∣2]Gm,n​(z,z′)=−δ(z−z′).

It is continuous across the source and material interfaces, has a derivative jump of −1-1−1 at z=z′z=z'z=z′, and satisfies outgoing or decaying conditions in the outer halfspaces. For a homogeneous medium,

Gm,n(z,z′)=−i2kz,mne−ikz,mn∣z−z′∣,kz,mn2=k02ϵˉ−∣Gm,n∣2,G_{m,n}(z,z')=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2k_{z,mn}} \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k_{z,mn}|z-z'|}, \qquad k_{z,mn}^2=k_0^2\bar\epsilon-|\mathbf G_{m,n}|^2,Gm,n​(z,z′)=−2kz,mn​i​e−ikz,mn​∣z−z′∣,kz,mn2​=k02​ϵˉ−∣Gm,n​∣2,

with the outgoing/decaying branch chosen for the stated time convention. The package uses the full layered kernel, including interface reflections. The homogeneous expression is useful for understanding the approximation used in the thesis comparison.

A basic wave produces a polarization source proportional to ξΘ0\xi\Theta_0ξΘ0​. The Green function propagates that source vertically; a second Fourier coefficient scatters the induced field back into a basic wave. The resulting overlaps have the form

∬Θ0∗(z) ξout(z)Gm,n(z,z′) ξin(z′)Θ0(z′) dz′dz.\iint \Theta_0^*(z)\,\xi_{\mathrm{out}}(z) G_{m,n}(z,z')\, \xi_{\mathrm{in}}(z')\Theta_0(z')\, \mathrm{d}z'\mathrm{d}z.∬Θ0∗​(z)ξout​(z)Gm,n​(z,z′)ξin​(z′)Θ0​(z′)dz′dz.

The receiver coefficient at zzz and source coefficient at z′z'z′ remain distinct, particularly when more than one layer is patterned. For auxiliary orders, the electric-field reconstruction also contains a local longitudinal term from the divergence constraint. Both the Green-mediated and local terms enter the high-order coupling.[3:1]

3. Assemble the coupling matrix

Projecting the electric-field equations onto Θ0\Theta_0Θ0​ gives

C=C1D+Crad+C2D.C=C_{\mathrm{1D}}+C_{\mathrm{rad}}+C_{\mathrm{2D}}.C=C1D​+Crad​+C2D​.

All three matrices have units of inverse length. The names describe the scattering paths:

  • C1DC_{\mathrm{1D}}C1D​: direct feedback between counterpropagating waves on the same axis, using Fourier orders such as (2,0)(2,0)(2,0) and (0,2)(0,2)(0,2).
  • CradC_{\mathrm{rad}}Crad​: scattering through the normal-radiation harmonic (0,0)(0,0)(0,0), giving both a frequency shift and radiative loss.
  • C2DC_{\mathrm{2D}}C2D​: coupling through the auxiliary orders m2+n2>1m^2+n^2>1m2+n2>1, including both their Green-mediated and local contributions.

For example, the direct coupling from SxS_xSx​ to RxR_xRx​ is

At Γ\GammaΓ in an infinite periodic structure, the envelope derivatives vanish and the problem reduces to

CV=ΛV,Λ=δ+iα.C\mathbf V=\Lambda\mathbf V,\qquad \Lambda=\delta+\mathrm{i}\alpha.CV=ΛV,Λ=δ+iα.

The four eigenvectors describe the four band-edge families. Here δ\deltaδ is the linearized Bragg detuning, conventionally δ=(β2−β02)/(2β0)≃β−β0\delta=(\beta^2-\beta_0^2)/(2\beta_0)\simeq\beta-\beta_0δ=(β2−β02​)/(2β0​)≃β−β0​, and α\alphaα is the amplitude-loss coefficient. The corresponding power-loss coefficient is 2α2\alpha2α. An open, radiating structure has a non-Hermitian CCC even when its constituent materials are lossless. Under the lossless-material, evanescent-auxiliary conditions, the nonradiative contributions are Hermitian.

4. Solve the finite-cavity envelopes

For a finite square aperture, the four amplitudes vary across the device:

ΛV=CV+i(∂xRx−∂xSx∂yRy−∂ySy).\Lambda\mathbf V=C\mathbf V+ \mathrm{i} \begin{pmatrix} \partial_xR_x\\ -\partial_xS_x\\ \partial_yR_y\\ -\partial_yS_y \end{pmatrix}.ΛV=CV+i​∂x​Rx​−∂x​Sx​∂y​Ry​−∂y​Sy​​​.

On 0≤x,y≤L0\le x,y\le L0≤x,y≤L, the no-incoming-wave boundaries are

Rx(0,y)=0,Sx(L,y)=0,Ry(x,0)=0,Sy(x,L)=0.R_x(0,y)=0,\qquad S_x(L,y)=0,\qquad R_y(x,0)=0,\qquad S_y(x,L)=0.Rx​(0,y)=0,Sx​(L,y)=0,Ry​(x,0)=0,Sy​(x,L)=0.

Outgoing waves remain free at the opposite edges. Setting every component to zero on every boundary would describe a different problem.

The solver places Rx,SxR_x,S_xRx​,Sx​ on the xxx faces of envelope cells and Ry,SyR_y,S_yRy​,Sy​ on the yyy faces. Face averages supply the cell-center fields, and face differences supply the derivatives. After eliminating the incoming boundary unknowns, the staggered discretization gives a sparse generalized eigenproblem

Av=ΛBv.A\mathbf v=\Lambda B\mathbf v.Av=ΛBv.

This grid resolves the cavity-scale variation, not every lattice period. A targeted shift-invert solve finds modes near a chosen complex shift; a single such search does not enumerate all competing modes.

5. Check the power balance

Define the envelope norm

P=∬ΩV†V dxdy.P=\iint_{\Omega}\mathbf V^\dagger\mathbf V\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.P=∬Ω​V†Vdxdy.

The outgoing edge flux is

Pedge=∫0L[∣Rx(L,y)∣2+∣Sx(0,y)∣2]dy+∫0L[∣Ry(x,L)∣2+∣Sy(x,0)∣2]dx.\begin{aligned} P_{\mathrm{edge}}={}&\int_0^L \left[|R_x(L,y)|^2+|S_x(0,y)|^2\right]\mathrm{d}y\\ &+\int_0^L \left[|R_y(x,L)|^2+|S_y(x,0)|^2\right]\mathrm{d}x. \end{aligned}Pedge​=​∫0L​[∣Rx​(L,y)∣2+∣Sx​(0,y)∣2]dy+∫0L​[∣Ry​(x,L)∣2+∣Sy​(x,0)∣2]dx.​

Taking the imaginary part of the integrated envelope equation gives

2αP=Pedge+2∬ΩIm⁡(V†CV) dxdy.2\alpha P=P_{\mathrm{edge}} +2\iint_{\Omega}\operatorname{Im} \left(\mathbf V^\dagger C\mathbf V\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.2αP=Pedge​+2∬Ω​Im(V†CV)dxdy.

When the auxiliary orders are evanescent and the materials are lossless, the volume term is the normal-radiation contribution. This identity separates edge leakage from surface radiation and provides an independent check of the finite discretization. With an added uniform modal gain, the threshold condition in power-gain units is Γgth=2α+αi\Gamma g_{\mathrm{th}}=2\alpha+\alpha_{\mathrm i}Γgth​=2α+αi​, where Γ\GammaΓ is the active-region confinement factor and αi\alpha_{\mathrm i}αi​ represents additional internal power loss.

Seven of the eight amplitude-loss thresholds differ by at most 1.3%. ET-C remains 6.98% lower than the thesis value, and the ET-C/D frequency positions also differ. The thesis table is printed to two decimal places, so its displayed precision is separate from numerical or physical uncertainty.

Cavity size and numerical refinement

CC and ET A/B branches for sampled sizes from 40 to 400 µm. Left: normalized frequency; right: αL\alpha LαL. The optical controls are D=10D=10D=10, q=32q=32q=32, F=64F=64F=64, with a finite-envelope grid of N=64N=64N=64. Dashed curves show the infinite-period comparison. A separate N=96N=96N=96 calculation checks the 400 µm endpoint.

The finite-mode frequencies approach their periodic band edges as the cavity grows. For ET, A has the lower threshold at 70 µm, while B has the lower threshold at 400 µm; the ordering exchanges between the sampled 100 and 200 µm points. The size-dependent balance of edge leakage and surface radiation therefore changes which band-edge family is favored. Here the plotted loss is αL\alpha LαL, so its size dependence includes the normalization by LLL.

Relative changes are plotted against the N=96N=96N=96 numerical reference at fixed D=10D=10D=10. That reference is not an exact continuum solution.

The maximum N=64→96N=64\to96N=64→96 change in αL\alpha LαL is 0.0368% for the eight tracked modes. Auxiliary-harmonic refinement is slower: in the Chapter 3 RIT case, B-mode radiation loss still changes by 2.93% at D=24→32D=24\to32D=24→32. For the introductory CC example, the selected mode’s αL\alpha LαL changes by 0.456% over that cutoff step. Envelope-grid refinement, vertical quadrature, and Fourier truncation control different errors and must be checked separately.

The full reproduction report includes the comparison tables, digitized reference points, power-balance checks, and remaining discrepancies. These results reproduce the main spectral and size-dependent trends, with the quantitative differences described above.
